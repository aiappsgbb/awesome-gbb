# Content planning

Build around what the audience needs to resolve, not everything the author knows.
Reuse the brief; ask only when an unanswered question changes the outcome.

## Audience and purpose

Record who is listening or reading, their prior knowledge, the question they bring,
and the change the presentation should enable. Distinguish a requested decision
from an outcome the presenter merely hopes for. A technical explanation need not
sell a product; a training deck should enable a skill, not just expose terminology.

Identify plausible objections from the supplied context, labeling inferred ones.
Avoid treating all executives as nontechnical or all engineers as detail-seeking.
For a mixed audience, keep a shared main argument and provide addressable detail.
Do not invent separate audience personas just to fill a brief.

### Confirm depth, not a stereotype

For a new deck or substantial restructure, record the audience/depth decision
and who supplied it: user, explicit brief or authorized session coordinator.
Do not reopen an already settled question. If the handoff conflicts with the
current request, surface that conflict before choosing the storyline.

When the answer is missing, ask one material question, such as "Is the primary
goal an executive decision, technical understanding, or a mixed audience with
one of those as the priority?" Use the available question tool or coordinator
channel. If neither can answer, label the structure provisional. Other supplied
facts may still be organized; do not manufacture audience confirmation.

| Orientation | Main narrative | Detail strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Executive | Decision, business consequence, options, concise evidence and risk | Include enough mechanism to make the recommendation credible; use backup for optional depth |
| Technical | Mental model, flow, assumptions, interfaces, examples and failure modes | Give depth where it resolves the technical question; split complexity, not every sentence |
| Mixed | Shared question and confirmed primary outcome | Put necessary shared context in the core and make specialist detail easy to reach |

Depth can change explanations, examples, density, sequence and slide count.
It does not justify exceeding the allotted time or turning every slide into a
document. An executive can be technically expert; a technical audience may
need a brief answer rather than an extended tutorial.

## Complete-deck defaults

Use these roles for a new complete presentation unless the user specifies a
different structure. They frame, rather than replace, the argument below.

| Role | Content | Adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| Cover | Meaningful title, confirmed presentation date, speaker or team attribution | Do not infer identity from the machine account or substitute generation date for event date |
| Introduction / framing | Problem setting, context, scope or audience question; why it matters | A short deck can combine context and recommendation; an agenda is not compulsory |
| Development | Purpose-specific reasoning, support, examples and material limits | Depth follows the confirmed audience and time, not a fixed template |
| Wrap-up / closing | Synthesis of the message and the appropriate decision, next step, learning check or unresolved issue | One slide can both summarize and close; an empty "Thank you" is not a substitute |

Missing date or attribution is an explicit metadata question, not permission to
invent it. Reuse confirmed information, and allow user-approved omissions. Draft
content work can proceed while metadata is pending; mark the gap in the brief
and resolve it or agree an omission before calling the deck finished.

Do not force four separate sections onto a tiny deck. Combine roles where
appropriate, keep cover/closing simple and avoid ornamental section breaks.
For slide excerpts, targeted revisions and rendering approved content, preserve
the existing scope rather than automatically adding opening/closing slides.
An appendix follows the main conclusion and is clearly backup material, not
an accidental continuation of the argument.

## Choose a structure

These are starting points, not mandatory sequences or slide counts.

| Purpose | Useful progression | Failure to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Executive decision | Recommendation -> reasons -> alternatives -> cost/risk -> specific decision | Hiding the request until the last slide; omitting the option of not acting |
| Technical explanation | Question -> mental model -> mechanism -> worked example -> boundaries | Component inventory without a flow, or unexplained acronyms |
| Persuasive proposal | Relevant problem -> credible change -> supporting evidence -> objections -> next step | Invented urgency, unsupported differentiation or a product feature dump |
| Training | Learning objective -> prerequisites -> explanation -> worked example -> practice -> feedback | A sales narrative instead of an opportunity to apply the idea |
| Progress update | Change since a named baseline -> implications -> causes/uncertainty -> blockers -> actions | Listing activity without showing what changed or what needs a decision |
| Exploratory discussion | Question -> what is known -> competing explanations -> evidence gaps -> next investigation | Pretending an open question has already been settled |

For decisions, consider leading with the answer. For explanations, introduce the
mental model needed to understand that answer. Use situation/problem/question
framing where it adds necessary context, not as a compulsory three-slide opening.
Stories and examples should illuminate a mechanism, consequence or choice;
do not force a hero's journey or suspense onto a neutral technical briefing.

## Develop a title-only storyline

Draft the central message in a complete sentence. It can be a conclusion, a
recommendation, a learning outcome or a clearly framed open question.
Group supporting reasons logically. A heading such as "Benefits" is a category,
not an explanation of why the recommendation follows.

Read the proposed titles in order without their bodies:

- Can the audience identify the question and the proposed answer?
- Does each step supply a needed premise, evidence, implication or action?
- Does a conclusion rely on an unstated assumption? Is an alternative dismissed
  without criteria? Are two slides doing the same work?
- Does the ending resolve the opening purpose, including what remains unknown?

The body of each slide must support its title; the title sequence must support
the overall message. Chronological source order and document section boundaries
are not evidence that the story is coherent.

## Select and allocate content

For each substantive slide, name the audience question it answers and its unique
contribution. Keep enough detail to explain the "why" or "how", not merely labels.
Turn abstract benefits into a mechanism plus a bounded consequence. Keep a
comparison fair by applying the same criteria to each option.

| Placement | What belongs there |
|---|---|
| Live slide | Message, essential support, interpretable labels, consequential qualifications |
| Speaker notes | Explanation, example, transition, full source locator and anticipated questions |
| Appendix | Supporting calculations, methods, detail for optional questions |
| Standalone reading | All context needed to understand the claim without a speaker, with visible references to further detail |

Never hide a limitation in the appendix if it changes the headline or decision.
For both live and reading delivery, maintain one factual basis and two appropriately
detailed views. Do not assume exported PDFs include notes.

Choose evidence displays by the relationship: trend, distribution, comparison,
sequence, dependency or trade-off. An architecture diagram should identify the
path being explained and relevant boundaries, not merely arrange product logos.
A demo should state what the observed action demonstrates and what it does not.

## Edit without flattening

Use concrete actors, actions and consequences. Define necessary terms once and
use them consistently. Preserve product names, version qualifiers, quotations
and numerical meaning across translation; do not add stronger claims in the
target language.

Remove redundancy, not distinct technical guarantees. Replace generic adjectives
with evidence or delete them. "Secure, scalable, innovative" is not an argument.
Text-only slides, tables and several supporting facts are valid when the task
needs them. Do not enforce a universal word count or visual quota.

Estimate time from explanation complexity, examples, demos, exercises and
discussion, not slide count. Mark it as an estimate until rehearsed. When time
is tight, shorten the argument deliberately and preserve required content;
do not solve the problem by reading faster or shrinking the font.
